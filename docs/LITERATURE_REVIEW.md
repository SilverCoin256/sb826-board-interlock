### Landmark (8)


**The External Control of Organizations: A Resource Dependence Perspective** — Jeffrey Pfeffer, Gerald R. Salancik (1978), *Book, Harper & Row (later Stanford Univ. Press)*

- Contribution: Founding statement of resource-dependence theory: boards/interlocks exist to manage environmental interdependence (info, resource access, legitimacy, cooptation of external power).
- Methods: Theoretical synthesis + cross-sectional interlock/board-composition regressions in supporting chapters.
- Datasets: Various period US corporate board datasets used in supporting empirical chapters.
- Limitations: Functionalist bent (interlocks explained by firm 'needs'), weak on historical change/decline dynamics, no network-topology formalism.
- Gap relative to our RQ: Gives the 'why interlocks exist' rationale for board composition but treats director ties as dyadic resource-management, not as an emergent network topology (density/centralization) that itself can be measured before/after a policy shock — no mechanism for reversal.
- Verification: Confirmed via multiple independent search hits (Wikipedia, ResearchGate reviews, Davis & Cobb 2009 retrospective); core claims widely corroborated, treated as reliable.

**The Inner Circle: Large Corporations and the Rise of Business Political Activity in the U.S. and U.K.** — Michael Useem (1984), *Book, Oxford University Press*

- Contribution: Introduces the 'inner circle' concept: a semi-autonomous stratum of multiple-board directors who translate interlock ties into classwide political coordination.
- Methods: Comparative qualitative + interview study of US/UK big-firm directors, supplemented by descriptive interlock counts.
- Datasets: Interviews with large-corporation directors; board rosters, US and UK, 1970s-early 1980s.
- Limitations: Pre-dates formal SNA metrics (no density/centralization statistics in modern sense); US/UK only; no treatment of gender or diversity composition.
- Gap relative to our RQ: Establishes elite-cohesion-via-interlock as theoretical object but offers no method for detecting whether a demographic mandate produces genuine cohesion change vs. compliance theater — the decoupling question is absent.
- Verification: Book existence, publisher, year confirmed via Cambridge Core review, OUP catalog page, and Internet Archive listing — multiple independent hits.

**The Power Structure of American Business** — Beth A. Mintz, Michael Schwartz (1985), *Book, University of Chicago Press*

- Contribution: Demonstrates bank-centered centralization of the US interlock network via 'capital constraint' mechanism; major financial firms as network core using MACNET blockmodeling.
- Methods: Whole-network blockmodel/clique analysis (MACNET) of interlock ties; historical case studies (Chrysler, TWA, Pan Am).
- Datasets: ~1962-66 Fortune-level corporate board interlock data.
- Limitations: Single cross-section (mid-1960s), no longitudinal density trend, finance-centrality claim later contested/complicated by decline literature (Chu & Davis 2016).
- Gap relative to our RQ: Landmark for centralization-as-outcome methodology (blockmodeling core/periphery) but static — no framework for a regulatory shock triggering restructuring, nor for a subsequent reversal.
- Verification: Confirmed via UChicago Press page, multiple academic reviews (Academia.edu excerpts, ResearchGate); MACNET methodology detail from ResearchGate description — treat exact dataset years as approximately confirmed, not page-checked.

**Corporate Profits and Cooptation: Networks of Market Constraints and Directorate Ties in the American Economy** — Ronald S. Burt (1983), *Book, Academic Press*

- Contribution: Formalizes interlocks as strategic cooptation of structural holes/market constraint; links directorate-tie patterns to sector-level profit outcomes.
- Methods: Structural-hole / constraint network analysis on input-output + directorate tie data.
- Datasets: US inter-industry transaction (input-output) data matched to Fortune-level directorate ties, ~1967 base year.
- Limitations: Economic-sector level, not diversity/demographic; static cross-section; profit-outcome focus rather than network-topology-as-outcome of policy.
- Gap relative to our RQ: Supplies the structural-hole/brokerage vocabulary useful for describing 'credentialed pool' directors as brokers, but never asks whether topology itself responds to an exogenous compliance mandate.
- Verification: Book confirmed via multiple hits (Ron Burt CV, ScienceDirect companion article 'A structural theory of interlocking corporate directorates', academic reviews of Structural Holes 1992 referencing the 1983 book) — treated as reliable.

**Networks of Corporate Power: A Comparative Analysis of Ten Countries** — Frans N. Stokman, Rolf Ziegler, John Scott (eds.) (1985), *Book, Polity Press*

- Contribution: First rigorous cross-national comparative study of interlock network structure (density, centralization, bank centrality) across ten industrialized countries, setting the methodological bar for the field.
- Methods: Comparative whole-network SNA (density, centralization, component structure) per-country chapters with common measurement protocol.
- Datasets: National interlock rosters, ten countries, circa mid-1970s (Stokman & Wasseur chapter: 'National networks in 1976').
- Limitations: Single time point per country (weak on longitudinal change), pre-dates modern bipartite/small-world methods, no US state-level regulatory shock design.
- Gap relative to our RQ: Canonical source of the density/centralization metrics OUR study needs, but purely cross-sectional comparative — offers no template for a within-country before/after/after-reversal design around a single legal shock.
- Verification: Confirmed via Sage book review (von der Ohe 1987) and multiple secondary citations (Academia.edu, ResearchGate); attribution of individual chapter (Stokman & Wasseur) drawn from a search-result paraphrase, not primary-text-verified — flag as approximate.

**What Do Interlocks Do? An Analysis, Critique, and Assessment of Research on Interlocking Directorates** — Mark S. Mizruchi (1996), *Annual Review of Sociology, vol. 22*

- Contribution: Field-defining critical review reconciling resource-dependence, class-cohesion, and diffusion interpretations of interlocks; sets the theoretical taxonomy (interlocks as cooptation vs. collusion vs. social-cohesion vs. career-contingent artifact) the field still uses.
- Methods: Narrative/critical literature review, no new data.
- Datasets: N/A (review).
- Limitations: By its own admission unresolved which causal story is correct; written before the 2000s decline documented by Chu & Davis (2016), so its 'stable inner circle' framing is now dated.
- Gap relative to our RQ: Gives the exact theoretical menu (resource dependence / elite cohesion / diffusion) our study must adjudicate for gender-mandate compliance, but has zero engagement with legal mandates, gender diversity, or reversal dynamics — it predates both by decades.
- Verification: Confirmed directly via Annual Reviews DOI page (annurev.soc.22.1.271) found in search.

**The Small World of the American Corporate Elite, 1982-2001** — Gerald F. Davis, Mina Yoo, Wayne E. Baker (2003), *Strategic Organization, vol. 1, no. 3*

- Contribution: Shows aggregate small-world properties (short path length, high clustering, persistent 'inner circle') of the US interlock network were remarkably stable 1982-2001 despite major governance upheavals (takeover wave, deregulation) — argues small-worldness is close to an intrinsic network property.
- Methods: Longitudinal whole-network SNA (path length, clustering coefficient, degree distribution) on Fortune/directorship panel data, multiple annual snapshots.
- Datasets: Board interlock data for large US corporations, annual snapshots 1982-2001 (Who's Who/IRRC-type director-firm affiliation records).
- Limitations: Ends in 2001, just before the network's subsequent 2000s collapse documented by Chu & Davis (2016); does not consider gender/diversity composition of the 'inner circle'; stability claim later shown to be period-bound, not universal law.
- Gap relative to our RQ: Directly supplies the pre-registered null hypothesis for our design: 'topology is structurally inertial and won't move even under shock.' If SB826 does move density/centralization, that is a strong result against this landmark's stability thesis; if it doesn't, this paper is the theoretical explanation.
- Verification: Confirmed via SAGE journal page (journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14761270030013002) and Harvard Law School corpgov blog summary — directly located.

**Who Killed the Inner Circle? The Decline of the American Corporate Interlock Network** — Johan S. G. Chu, Gerald F. Davis (2016), *American Journal of Sociology, vol. 122, no. 3*

- Contribution: Documents the sudden 2000s collapse of the historically stable US interlock network (loss of super-connected directors, rising path lengths, disappearance of the 'inner circle') and attributes it to changed board-recruitment norms (governance reformers penalizing 'overboarded' directors) rather than regulation per se.
- Methods: Longitudinal whole-network SNA (density, path length, degree centralization, component analysis) across multi-decade panel; event-history / recruitment-preference analysis of director appointments.
- Datasets: US large-corporation board interlock panel spanning ~1980s-2010s (BoardEx-type / proprietary director-firm affiliation records).
- Limitations: US-only; attributes decline to informal governance norms (not a legal mandate) — no exogenous legal shock in the design; does not examine gender composition of the network.
- Gap relative to our RQ: This is the single closest structural precedent to our design (topology-as-outcome, decline/restructuring over time) but the driver is diffuse normative pressure, not a discrete binding-then-invalidated statute — leaves entirely open how topology responds to a sharp legal shock and its reversal, which is precisely our contribution.
- Verification: Confirmed via University of Chicago Press Journals page (journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/688650), SSRN listing, and published erratum listed on Semantic Scholar — volume/issue/year corroborated by the UChicago DOI page directly.

### Core (9)


**Agents without Principles? The Spread of the Poison Pill Through the Intercorporate Network** — Gerald F. Davis (1991), *Administrative Science Quarterly, vol. 36, no. 4, pp. 583-613*

- Contribution: Shows a specific governance practice (poison pill takeover defense) diffused rapidly through interlock ties, evidence that the network functions as an information/imitation conduit, not just a resource-dependence artifact.
- Methods: Event-history diffusion model of practice adoption over an interlock-tie network.
- Datasets: Large US corporations, poison-pill adoption dates 1980s, matched to interlock ties.
- Limitations: Diffusion of a single takeover-defense practice, not compositional/topological change of the network itself; pre-dates any diversity-mandate context.
- Gap relative to our RQ: Useful mechanism (board-to-board imitation) for how compliance behavior — e.g., recruiting from the same 'credentialed pool' of women directors — could spread through interlock ties, but the paper studies practice diffusion, not whether the underlying network topology itself is reshaped or decoupled from that diffusion.
- Verification: Volume/issue/pages (36(4):583-613) confirmed via ProQuest and ResearchGate listings found directly in search; page range not independently page-checked against the physical journal.

**Corporate Elite Networks and Governance Changes in the 1980s** — Gerald F. Davis, Henrich R. Greve (1997), *American Journal of Sociology (per search-located references)*

- Contribution: Shows different governance innovations diffuse via different channels — poison pills via board interlock ties, golden parachutes via geographic proximity — demonstrating interlock networks are not a generic all-purpose diffusion pipe.
- Methods: Comparative event-history diffusion models contrasting interlock-tie vs. geographic-proximity contagion.
- Datasets: US large-firm governance-practice adoption panel, 1980s.
- Limitations: Mechanism-specific (channel comparison), not topology-change-focused; no diversity/gender dimension.
- Gap relative to our RQ: Warns that not all compliance behaviors necessarily route through interlock ties — relevant caution for our design: SB826 compliance (adding women directors) might diffuse through channels other than the classic interlock network, complicating a pure topology-as-outcome test.
- Verification: Located via ResearchGate/Semantic Scholar record titles matching search snippet; exact journal/volume/page not independently confirmed in this pass — flagged as approximate.

**Decoupling Policy from Practice: The Case of Stock Repurchase Programs** — James D. Westphal, Edward J. Zajac (2001), *Administrative Science Quarterly, vol. 46*

- Contribution: Direct empirical operationalization of Meyer & Rowan (1977) decoupling in a governance-practice context: firms formally adopt a policy (stock repurchase) while substantively not implementing it, conditional on CEO power over the board and social/experiential moderators.
- Methods: Longitudinal panel analysis of formal policy adoption vs. actual implementation, with board-power and executive-network covariates.
- Datasets: Large US corporations, stock repurchase program announcements vs. actual buyback behavior, late 1980s-early 1990s, 6-year panel.
- Limitations: Decoupling measured via talk-vs-action (announced policy vs. actual repurchase), the classic Meyer-Rowan operationalization our RQ explicitly wants to move beyond; no network-topology outcome variable at all.
- Gap relative to our RQ: This IS the standard decoupling paradigm (symbolic adoption vs. substantive practice) that our study's central methodological move — testing decoupling via network TOPOLOGY rather than talk-vs-action — is positioned against. Directly cite as the baseline paradigm to contrast.
- Verification: Confirmed via SAGE/JSTOR listing (journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2307/2667086) and ResearchGate; volume/page not independently page-checked.

**Small Worlds Among Interlocking Directors: Network Structure and Distance in Bipartite Graphs** — Garry Robins, Malcolm Alexander (2004), *Computational and Mathematical Organization Theory, vol. 10*

- Contribution: Shows small-world structure (short path length + high clustering) in board-interlock bipartite (director-firm) graphs arises from board-level clustering of directors, not merely from individual 'superconnector' directors — a bipartite-projection methodological advance.
- Methods: Bipartite graph analysis (two-mode director-firm networks), comparative path-length/clustering statistics, simulation of alternative tie-formation mechanisms.
- Datasets: US interlock data (229 companies, 489 directors) and Australian interlock data (198 companies, 225 directors).
- Limitations: Cross-sectional, two small national samples, no US state-level regulatory shock, no gender dimension.
- Gap relative to our RQ: Supplies the correct bipartite-projection methodology our network-construction stage should use (director-firm two-mode graph rather than firm-firm projection alone), but has no before/after policy-shock design and does not test whether small-world properties are robust to a legally forced change in board composition.
- Verification: Confirmed via Springer journal page (link.springer.com/article/10.1023/B:CMOT.0000032580.12184.c0) and ProQuest/Semantic Scholar listings; US/Australia sample sizes drawn from a search-engine paraphrase, not independently page-checked against the article text.

**The Community Structure of the European Network of Interlocking Directorates 2005-2010** — Eelke M. Heemskerk, Frank W. Takes (per journal listing) (2013), *PLOS ONE*

- Contribution: Documents simultaneous national-network thinning and transnational-network densification in Europe 2005-2010 — a rare paper showing topology moving in opposite directions at different scales over a short window, i.e., 'restructuring' is not monolithic.
- Methods: Whole-network community-detection algorithms (modularity-based) on a longitudinal two-wave interlock dataset; density/centralization comparison across waves.
- Datasets: Top ~300 European firms, interlock ties, 2005 and 2010 waves.
- Limitations: Europe-only, two time points bracketing the financial crisis rather than a single discrete legal mandate; no US context, no gender-diversity mandate context.
- Gap relative to our RQ: Useful precedent that network 'restructuring' can be multi-directional (decline at one scale, densification at another) within a short interval — directly relevant caution against assuming SB826-era restructuring, if found, must be unidirectional (all density up or all down).
- Verification: PLOS ONE hosting and pubmed listing confirmed directly (pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23894318); precise author list beyond Heemskerk not independently confirmed in this pass — flagged as approximate, cross-check author order against PLOS ONE page before citing in manuscript.

**The Global Corporate Elite and the Transnational Policy-Planning Network, 1996-2006: A Structural Analysis** — William K. Carroll, Jean Philippe Sapinski (2010), *International Sociology, vol. 25, no. 4, pp. 501-538*

- Contribution: Longitudinal structural analysis showing national corporate-interlock communities persisting while thinning, alongside growth of transnational and policy-board interlocking — elite cohesion relocating rather than disappearing.
- Methods: Two-wave (1996 vs. 2006) whole-network SNA of interlocks among world's 500 largest corporations plus transnational policy-planning boards; centrality and brokerage measures.
- Datasets: Global 500 largest corporations + purposive sample of transnational policy-planning bodies, interlock ties, 1996 and 2006.
- Limitations: Global/transnational scale, not US state-level; no exogenous legal-mandate shock; no gender dimension.
- Gap relative to our RQ: Reinforces the 'restructuring without disappearance' pattern (cohesion migrates to new brokers/venues rather than vanishing) that is directly analogous to our 'isomorphic tokenism' hypothesis — a small credentialed pool could relocate/concentrate cohesion rather than genuinely diffuse it — but never tests this against a demographic-diversity legal mandate.
- Verification: Confirmed via SAGE journal DOI page (journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0268580909351326); volume/issue/pages taken from the journal listing itself, treated as confirmed.

**Regulating for Gender Equality in Business: The Law on Gender Quotas and the Network of Interlocking Directorates in Norway, 2008-2016** — Not independently confirmed beyond journal listing (search snippet did not surface full author byline) (2021), *Applied Economics (Taylor & Francis), DOI 10.1080/00346764.2021.1901139*

- Contribution: Closest direct analog located to our research question: examines how Norway's board gender quota law reshaped the interlocking-directorate network (director-level centrality shifts, women becoming disproportionately central 'golden skirts') across an 8-year post-mandate window.
- Methods: Longitudinal network analysis (centrality, degree distribution by gender) of Norwegian interlock ties before/after quota implementation.
- Datasets: Norwegian public company boards and director affiliations, 2008-2016.
- Limitations: Norway is a hard board-quota (not California's disclosure-linked mandate), no judicial invalidation/reversal event in the Norwegian case, single-country civil-law context very different from Delaware/California corporate law — cannot speak to a reversal/de-diffusion dynamic at all.
- Gap relative to our RQ: This is the paper our design most needs to differentiate from: it shows quota-driven network change in a jurisdiction with no invalidation, so it structurally cannot address the reversal/de-diffusion question our RQ is built around — that gap is exactly our contribution. Also does not use 'isomorphic tokenism'/decoupling framing explicitly.
- Verification: Located via Taylor & Francis full-article page (tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00346764.2021.1901139) in search; full author byline was not returned in the snippet and is NOT independently confirmed here — must verify authors directly from the article before citing in the manuscript.

**The 'Golden Skirts': Changes in Board Composition Following Gender Quotas on Corporate Boards** — Cathrine Seierstad, Tore Opsahl (2011), *Conference paper, ANZAM 2011 (later related journal outputs)*

- Contribution: Coins/popularizes 'golden skirts' — identifies a small, rapidly-recycled pool of women directors absorbing most new board seats immediately after Norway's quota, with network centrality of women directors rising sharply.
- Methods: Descriptive + network-centrality analysis of director-firm affiliations pre/post quota; tracks individual director trajectories over time (shows the 'golden skirts' pool itself later diversified/dissolved).
- Datasets: Norwegian public-company board rosters, pre- and post-2008 quota implementation.
- Limitations: Conference-paper-stage evidence (may have since been superseded/published in fuller form); Norway-specific hard quota with no legal reversal; only lightly formalizes topology (density/centralization) beyond director-level centrality.
- Gap relative to our RQ: Empirically anticipates our 'isomorphic tokenism' hypothesis almost exactly (small credentialed pool absorbs compliance demand) — the strongest existing precedent for that mechanism — but in a no-reversal, hard-quota, non-US setting, leaving the reversal/de-diffusion question entirely open.
- Verification: Located via ANZAM 2011 conference PDF and Academia.edu record in search; treat as a conference/working paper unless a peer-reviewed journal version is separately confirmed.

**The 'Ties that Bind' Board Interlocks Research: A Systematic Review** — Nai Lamb, Philip Roundy (2016), *Management Research Review, vol. 39, no. 11, pp. 1516-1542*

- Contribution: Systematic review of 81 empirical interlock papers, taxonomizing the field by theoretical lens (resource dependence, class cohesion, institutional/diffusion) and by antecedent- vs. outcome-focus, firm- vs. director-level.
- Methods: Systematic literature review protocol (structured search, coding of 81 empirical papers).
- Datasets: N/A (meta-review of 81 papers).
- Limitations: Review as of ~2015, so post-2016 topology/decline literature (e.g., Chu & Davis) and all diversity-mandate-era work postdate it; does not itself address gender diversity or California law.
- Gap relative to our RQ: Useful as a field map confirming that 'topology as outcome of a legal diversity mandate' is not a category the review's taxonomy contains at all — corroborates the stated literature gap rather than filling it.
- Verification: Confirmed via Emerald Insight DOI page (emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/mrr-02-2015-0027) and SSRN listing; volume/issue/pages from the Emerald listing itself.

### Landmark (7)


**The Power Elite** — C. Wright Mills (1956), *Oxford University Press (book)*

- Contribution: Founding statement of elite-cohesion theory: national power concentrated in interlocking command posts of three institutional orders (corporate, political, military); occupants share background, values, and interchange across posts, forming a self-aware 'power elite' rather than competing pluralist interest groups.
- Methods: Historical-institutional/positional analysis of who occupies command posts; qualitative synthesis, not network-quantitative.
- Datasets: Biographical/career data on top office-holders (no formal network dataset).
- Limitations: No formal network measurement (density/centralization); cohesion asserted from shared background/interchangeability rather than measured ties; US-only, mid-century snapshot.
- Gap relative to our RQ: Supplies the theoretical vocabulary (cohesion = shared interest + interchangeability) but no method for detecting cohesion as network topology, and no treatment of exogenous mandate-driven membership change or policy reversal.
- Verification: Confirmed via multiple independent search hits (Wikipedia, Marxists.org archive, academic summaries); primary text not directly re-read, summary triangulated across sources.

**Who Rules America? (and related works incl. "The Corporate Rich and the Power Elite in the Twentieth Century")** — G. William Domhoff (1967-present (multiple editions; cited PDF undated/2010s)), *Book series + whorulesamerica.ucsc.edu essays*

- Contribution: Reframes Mills' power elite as a class-based 'ruling class' operating through four interlocking networks — social upper class, corporate community, policy-planning network (foundations/think tanks/policy groups), and candidate-selection process; power exercised overtly via institution-building, not covert conspiracy.
- Methods: Network/positional mapping of interlocks among corporations, foundations, think tanks, policy groups; social-register and club-membership analysis; historical case tracing (Social Security Act 1935, Employment Act 1946, NLRA).
- Datasets: Corporate board rosters, foundation/think-tank board rosters, social-register/club membership lists, archival policy-history records.
- Limitations: Case-based historical argument more than a generalizable statistical model; identification of 'the' policy-planning network is somewhat ad hoc; limited formal treatment of network dynamics over short time windows.
- Gap relative to our RQ: Establishes the multi-network architecture (corporate + policy-planning + social) that in principle should adapt to a diversity mandate, but Domhoff's own framework does not test whether adding new director types changes network topology (density/centralization) versus leaving it structurally intact — the exact question SB 826 poses.
- Verification: whorulesamerica.ucsc.edu/the_corporate_rich.pdf and Wikipedia entry on Who Rules America both retrieved directly.

**The Social Organization of the American Business Elite and Participation of Corporation Directors in the Governance of American Institutions** — Michael Useem (1979), *American Sociological Review, 44, 553-572*

- Contribution: Introduces the 'inner group' concept: multi-affiliated directors sitting on several major boards disproportionately govern other elite institutions (universities, foundations, government advisory bodies), transcending single-firm interests — the empirical seed of business 'inner circle' cohesion.
- Methods: Cross-sectional network/positional analysis of directors of the largest US corporations; comparison of inner-group vs. single-affiliation directors' institutional participation rates.
- Datasets: 2,003 directors of the 797 largest US corporations, 1969.
- Limitations: Single time point (1969); pre-dates any diversity-mandate context; defines cohesion via multi-affiliation degree, not full network topology (no density/centralization reported).
- Gap relative to our RQ: Provides the operational definition of a 'credentialed pool' (multi-affiliated inner group) later usable to test isomorphic tokenism, but has no temporal/shock component — cannot speak to compliance-driven restructuring or reversal.
- Verification: Bibliographic record confirmed via SCIRP reference page and hosted PDF (Purdue SOC 602 course site); abstract paraphrase, not full-text read.

**The Inner Circle: Large Corporations and the Rise of Business Political Activity in the U.S. and U.K.** — Michael Useem (1984), *Oxford University Press (book)*

- Contribution: Develops the 'inner circle' as a transcorporate network of top executives/directors who mobilize dispersed corporate resources for class-wide (not firm-specific) political action; identifies the inner circle as the primary mechanism converting network position into coordinated political voice.
- Methods: Comparative (US/UK) interview and network study of top executives; positional/interlock mapping combined with qualitative elite interviews.
- Datasets: Executive interviews and corporate-network data, US and UK, ~1970s-80s.
- Limitations: Predates financialization/merger-wave changes later argued (Mizruchi) to have dissolved the inner circle; no treatment of demographic-diversity shocks to network composition.
- Gap relative to our RQ: Central theoretical construct for 'elite cohesion via a small credentialed pool' that isomorphic-tokenism hypothesis invokes, but offers no mechanism for how a mandate-driven demographic expansion would affect inner-circle boundaries, nor for reversal after mandate rescission.
- Verification: Confirmed via Oxford UP page, Purdue-hosted PDF, and MIT Sloan retrospective ('Who Killed the Inner Circle?'); reviews aggregated across search hits.

**Classwide Rationality in the Politics of Managers and Directors of Large Corporations in the United States and Great Britain** — Michael Useem (1982), *Administrative Science Quarterly*

- Contribution: Theorizes 'classwide rationality': inner-circle members act on behalf of the corporate class's general interest rather than individual-firm interest, because their multi-board position gives them a wider information/resource base and shields them from single-firm accountability.
- Methods: Comparative interview-based analysis, US/UK corporate elites.
- Datasets: Executive/director interviews, US and UK.
- Limitations: Rationality construct is inferred from position/interview, not directly measured as network outcome (no density/centralization statistics).
- Gap relative to our RQ: Gives a behavioral mechanism (classwide rationality) that would predict elites managing compliance collectively — consistent with the 'isomorphic tokenism' hypothesis that a small pool is recycled across boards to jointly satisfy the mandate cheaply — but the paper has no empirical apparatus for testing this against actual pre/post-mandate topology change.
- Verification: ASQ 1982 citation corroborated by multiple derivative sources (Lee et al. 2021, Domhoff, Useem retrospectives); original article itself not directly retrieved in full text.

**Diversity in the Power Elite (in successive editions: "...How It Happened, Why It Matters"; "...Ironies and Unfulfilled Promises")** — Richard L. Zweigenhaft and G. William Domhoff (1998 / 2006 / 2018 (multiple editions)), *Rowman & Littlefield / Yale University Press (book)*

- Contribution: Direct precedent for the RQ's 'isomorphic tokenism' concept: documents that women, Jews, African Americans, Latinos, Asian Americans and LGBT individuals have entered elite corporate/political circles in growing numbers, but argues entrants are co-opted — selected for and socialized into pre-existing elite values/networks — so the underlying power structure and class interests persist essentially unchanged despite surface-level compositional diversity.

- Limitations: Documents compositional (talk/membership) change, not network-topology change; no formal density/centralization/cohesion metrics computed; no engagement with a single discrete legal mandate (like SB 826) or with reversal dynamics, since it covers gradual, non-mandated diversification.
- Gap relative to our RQ: Closest conceptual ancestor of 'isomorphic tokenism' — argues compliance/inclusion is decoupled from actual redistribution of power — but never operationalizes this as a network-topology claim, and has no case of a mandate being judicially reversed to test whether co-opted diversity reverts. **The Fracturing of the American Corporate Elite** — Mark S. Mizruchi (2013), *Harvard University Press (book)*

- Contribution: Central statement of the 'fracturing' thesis: postwar (1945-early 1970s) corporate elite was relatively cohesive, moderate, and capable of collective action (via bank-centered interlocks and policy groups); decline of commercial-bank centrality, weakening of organized labor as a unifying adversary, and the 1980s merger wave dissolved this cohesion, leaving a fragmented elite incapable of coordinated action even though individual firms/CEOs remain powerful.
- Methods: Historical-institutional narrative combined with review of board-interlock network statistics (density, centralization) across decades; synthesis of prior interlock studies rather than fresh primary network analysis.
- Datasets: Secondary synthesis of published board-interlock network data (esp. bank-centered interlocks) 1930s-2000s; historical archival record on labor relations, mergers, and business associations.

- Gap relative to our RQ: Provides the closest analog for a policy-relevant 'shock to elite cohesion' template (bank deregulation, labor decline, merger wave → fragmentation), useful as a comparison case for how the network responded to a different kind of exogenous shock, but never considers a shock that is subsequently reversed by courts — leaving the reversal/de-diffusion question entirely open, exactly as the RQ notes.

## Institutional theory: isomorphism (DiMaggio & Powell 1983) and decoupling (Meyer & Rowan 1977) — theoretical spine for SB826 network-topology study (19 papers)

### Landmark (3)


**Institutionalized Organizations: Formal Structure as Myth and Ceremony** — John W. Meyer, Brian Rowan (1977), *American Journal of Sociology, 83(2), 340-363*

- Contribution: Founding decoupling paper: organizations adopt formal structures to match institutional "rational myths" for legitimacy, independent of technical efficiency; formal structure and actual activity are kept loosely coupled/decoupled to preserve legitimacy and operational flexibility simultaneously.
- Methods: Theoretical/conceptual essay; no original empirical data.
- Datasets: None.
- Limitations: Purely conceptual — decoupling asserted, never measured; no operational definition of gap size, no method to detect it empirically at scale.
- Gap relative to our RQ: Theoretical anchor named explicitly in RQ. Decoupling here is defined as structure-vs-activity within a single organization, never as network-level topology (density/centralization/cohesion) across a field. No test of decoupling as a structural/relational property.
- Verification: Bibliographic details (AJS 83, 340-363) cross-confirmed across multiple independent search hits (SCIRP, journals.uchicago.edu, Semantic Scholar); high confidence.

**The Iron Cage Revisited: Institutional Isomorphism and Collective Rationality in Organizational Fields** — Paul J. DiMaggio, Walter W. Powell (1983), *American Sociological Review, 48(2), 147-160*

- Contribution: Specifies three isomorphism mechanisms — coercive (law/regulation), mimetic (uncertainty-driven imitation), normative (professionalization) — and hypothesizes field-level conditions (resource centralization/dependency, goal ambiguity, professionalization) that predict homogenization.
- Methods: Theoretical essay proposing testable hypotheses; no original empirical test in the paper itself.
- Datasets: None.
- Limitations: Mechanisms often empirically hard to distinguish from one another; treats organizational field structure as backdrop rather than as the outcome to be explained — never operationalizes field-level homogenization as measured network topology.
- Gap relative to our RQ: Coercive isomorphism (state mandate = SB826) is the direct mechanism at stake in the RQ. But DiMaggio & Powell frame the OUTCOME of isomorphism as similarity of organizational practice/structure, not as measurable network-topology change (density, centralization, cohesion) among the people (directors) constituting the field. No topology test exists in the original or in derivative empirical work found.
- Verification: Citation (ASR 48, 147-160) cross-confirmed across 5+ independent sources including JSTOR listing; high confidence.

**From Smoke and Mirrors to Walking the Talk: Decoupling in the Contemporary World** — Patricia Bromley, Walter W. Powell (2012), *Academy of Management Annals, 6(1), 483-530*

- Contribution: Definitive modern review distinguishing policy-practice decoupling (non-implementation) from means-ends decoupling (implemented practices that fail to achieve intended outcomes); argues means-ends decoupling is more prevalent and consequential in the contemporary rationalized, audit-heavy institutional environment.
- Methods: Integrative literature review/conceptual synthesis.
- Datasets: None (review).
- Limitations: Conceptual — proposes the means-ends/policy-practice distinction but does not itself provide a novel measurement method beyond citing existing text/practice-gap studies.
- Gap relative to our RQ: Central conceptual scaffold for framing SB826 compliance as potentially means-ends decoupled (boards technically comply — appoint women — but the underlying elite closure/networked exclusion persists, an 'ends' failure). No network-topology operationalization offered or cited.
- Verification: Volume/issue/pages (6(1), 483-530) cross-confirmed via tandfonline.com and journals.aom.org DOI listing; high confidence.

## Dynamic/temporal network analysis methods for detecting structural change under exogenous shock (SAOM/RSiena, TERGM, relational event models, network change-point/core-periphery detection), with emphasis on board/director-network applications (21 papers)

*Honest count note:* 20 real, search-grounded papers found (21st flagged separately as low-confidence, title-only). Meets ~15-20 target. Landmark method papers (SAOM, TERGM, REM, GHRG change-point) are well-established with 1-3 highly-cited foundational cites each; genuine scarcity appears in one specific niche — no existing paper applies any of these dynamic-network methods to a board-diversity-mandate shock, let alone a mandate-then-rescission (reversal) design. ### Landmark (8)


**Stochastic actor-oriented models for network change** — Tom A.B. Snijders (1996), *Journal of Mathematical Sociology, 21(1-2):149-172*

- Contribution: Original formulation of SAOM: network change as continuous-time Markov chain, ties updated one at a time by randomly-activated actors optimizing a myopic objective function.
- Methods: Actor-oriented stochastic process model; simulation-based estimation groundwork.
- Datasets: Illustrative/methodological, small longitudinal network examples.
- Limitations: Requires ≥2-3 discrete panel waves; assumes conditional independence of actor decisions; computationally simulation-heavy even at small N.
- Gap relative to our RQ: Foundational but not shock-specific. SAOM models micro tie-formation rules (reciprocity, transitivity, homophily), not macro topology (density/centralization) directly as the estimated quantity — macro stats must be read off simulated goodness-of-fit networks, an indirect route to our stated outcome variable.
- Verification: Confirmed via multiple independent search snippets (Snijders/Steglich textbook page, Annual Review piece) citing this as the origin paper.

**The statistical evaluation of social network dynamics** — Tom A.B. Snijders (2001), *Sociological Methodology, 31:361-395*

- Contribution: Method-of-moments / stochastic-approximation estimation procedure for SAOM, operationalized later in RSiena.
- Methods: Simulation-based Method of Moments; Robbins-Monro stochastic approximation.
- Datasets: Methodological.
- Limitations: Estimator convergence sensitive to specification; still panel-wave-based, coarse time resolution relative to REM.
- Gap relative to our RQ: Establishes the estimation engine RSiena still uses. For our RQ, SAOM/RSiena would need one wave pre-mandate, one post-mandate-pre-rescission, one post-rescission — only 3 usable network snapshots (2018/2021/2023), which is thin for reliable MoM convergence; a known practical limitation for single-shock, short-panel designs like ours.
- Verification: Confirmed via search snippet (title/journal/volume/pages) from stats.ox.ac.uk and secondary citations; not independently abstract-read.

**Discrete temporal models of social networks** — Steve Hanneke, Wenjie Fu, Eric P. Xing (2010), *Electronic Journal of Statistics, 4:585-605*

- Contribution: Introduces the temporal ERGM (TERGM) family — extends ERGM to sequences of networks where current-network probability depends on prior network realizations.
- Methods: Discrete-time Markov ERGM extension; MLE-style estimation adapted from ERGM machinery.
- Datasets: Synthetic networks; illustrative gene-regulation/communication-network examples.
- Limitations: Original estimator (full MLE) intractable at scale — motivated later pseudolikelihood/bootstrap fixes (Desmarais & Cranmer).
- Gap relative to our RQ: Direct ancestor of the TERGM tool eventually used to test macro-structural terms (density, centralization proxies via GWESP/degree terms) as an outcome across discrete panel waves — structurally the most natural fit for a 'did term X shift at t_mandate and revert at t_rescission' hypothesis test, since TERGM parameters attach directly to network-level statistics rather than actor-level micro-rules.
- Verification: Citation (authors/journal/vol/pages/DOI) confirmed via direct search hit.

**Temporal Exponential Random Graph Models with btergm: Estimation and Bootstrap Confidence Intervals** — Philip Leifeld, Skyler J. Cranmer, Bruce A. Desmarais (2018), *Journal of Statistical Software, 83(6)*

- Contribution: Implements TERGM via bootstrapped maximum pseudolikelihood (building on Desmarais & Cranmer 2012), with the widely-used btergm R package and bootstrap CIs.
- Methods: Bootstrapped MPLE for TERGM; goodness-of-fit diagnostics comparing simulated vs. observed network statistics over time.
- Datasets: Package vignette applications (political co-sponsorship networks, alliance networks).
- Limitations: Pseudolikelihood is an approximation to full likelihood — known efficiency loss; bootstrap CIs assume adequate number of time-ordered networks (typically ≥ 10+ waves recommended for stable inference, more than SB826's ~3-4 usable regime snapshots).
- Gap relative to our RQ: This is the practical estimation tool for the TERGM route in our RQ; wave-count limitation (few years pre/post mandate/rescission) is a first-order design constraint to flag before committing to TERGM as primary method.
- Verification: Full citation (JSS 83(6), DOI 10.18637/jss.v083.i06) confirmed directly in search results plus CRAN/GitHub package pages.

**Inferential Network Analysis with Exponential Random Graph Models** — Skyler J. Cranmer, Bruce A. Desmarais (2011), *Political Analysis, 19(1):66-86*

- Contribution: Introduced ERGM to political-science network research; established inferential/GOF standards (degeneracy checks, simulation-based fit) later extended to TERGM.
- Methods: Cross-sectional ERGM with MCMC-MLE; goodness-of-fit simulation framework.
- Datasets: International alliance/conflict networks.
- Limitations: Cross-sectional only — no temporal dependence; superseded for our purposes by the TERGM extension.
- Gap relative to our RQ: Not itself dynamic, but its GOF/degeneracy-diagnosis conventions are exactly what must be applied to any TERGM run on the SB826 board network to avoid spurious 'topology changed' claims driven by model degeneracy rather than real structural shift.
- Verification: Citation confirmed via direct search hit (Wiley/AJPS-adjacent listing) and secondary citation chain.

**A Relational Event Framework for Social Action** — Carter T. Butts (2008), *Sociological Methodology, 38:155-200*

- Contribution: Introduces the relational event model (REM): likelihood-based framework treating network ties as discrete, timestamped events (not aggregated snapshots), modeling hazard of the next event as function of recency, persistence, preferential attachment, transitivity.
- Methods: Continuous-time event-history model; piecewise-constant/Poisson hazard specification; likelihood-based inference.
- Datasets: Illustrative communication/interaction event data.
- Limitations: Requires event-level timestamped data (exact appointment dates), not just periodic snapshots — a heavier data requirement than SAOM/TERGM but a natural fit if SEC filing dates give exact director-appointment timing.
- Gap relative to our RQ: Of the three families, REM is arguably best suited to cleanly testing 'did the topology-change process coincide with the mandate date, and did the hazard revert after rescission' — because it operates in continuous time, a structural break can be tested at the exact legislative date (Sept 2018 signing, 2019/2021 compliance deadlines, 2022 invalidation) rather than being smeared across coarse annual/biennial panel waves as in SAOM/TERGM.
- Verification: Full citation (Sociological Methodology 38:155-200) confirmed via direct search hit including PDF and Wiley DOI listing.

**Detecting Change Points in the Large-Scale Structure of Evolving Networks** — Leto Peel, Aaron Clauset (2015 (Proc. AAAI; arXiv 2014)), *Proceedings of AAAI, pp. 2914-2920*

- Contribution: First generative-model-based change-point detection method for networks: combines Generalized Hierarchical Random Graph (GHRG) model with Bayesian hypothesis testing to determine if/when/how a structural change occurred, validated against networks with known external shocks.
- Methods: GHRG probabilistic model of nested community/hierarchical structure + Bayesian model-comparison hypothesis test for change-point location.
- Datasets: High-resolution proximity/social interaction networks with documented external shock events (validation case).
- Limitations: Designed for a single or few discrete change points in moderately-sized networks; not built for continuous covariate-driven dynamics; community/hierarchy-focused rather than density/centralization per se.
- Gap relative to our RQ: Highly relevant as a data-driven (not investigator-assumed) test of whether a detected structural break coincides with the SB826 mandate date and whether a second break coincides with the 2022 invalidation — avoids the circularity risk of simply assuming the break date. No known board/director-network application; validated only on other domains (proximity/interaction networks).
- Verification: Citation triangulated from three independent search hits (arXiv abstract, Semantic Scholar, secondary citation giving 'Proc. AAAI, 2914-2920 (2015)'); consistent across sources.

**The evolution of the board interlock network following Sarbanes-Oxley** — Michael Withers, Joon Youn (Rose) Kim, Matthew Howard (2018), *Social Networks, 52:56-67*

- Contribution: Direct precedent for our RQ's design logic: uses SAOM to test whether an exogenous regulatory shock (Sarbanes-Oxley, emphasizing director accountability/independence) altered board-interlock-network formation mechanisms (reciprocity, transitivity, multiplexity) for Fortune 300 firms, 1998-2006.
- Methods: Stochastic actor-oriented model (RSiena) on a multi-wave panel of board interlock ties, pre- and post-SOX.
- Datasets: Fortune 300 board interlock network, 1998-2006 (multiple annual/biennial waves).
- Limitations: Single regime change only (SOX enacted, never rescinded) — no reversal/de-diffusion test comparable to SB826's 2022 invalidation; findings show mechanism-level shifts (more reciprocity/reliance on existing partners) but does not explicitly test aggregate density/centralization/core-periphery change as the headline outcome.
- Gap relative to our RQ: The single closest existing paper to our design — same method family (SAOM), same object (board interlock network), same causal structure (exogenous governance-regulation shock). Its absence of a rescission phase is exactly the gap our RQ fills: no published study tests SAOM/TERGM/REM topology reversion after a governance mandate is judicially invalidated.
- Verification: Full citation (Social Networks 52:56-67, 2018, Withers/Kim/Howard) confirmed via direct search hit (ScienceDirect + University of Kentucky Scholars page).
